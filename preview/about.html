About

A Gospel atlas.

The Way follows the life of Jesus from the promise of John’s birth in the temple to the Ascension on the Mount of Olives and the coming of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost, in 90 scenes. Each scene sets a moment from the Gospels on the map where it happened.

Scroll, and the camera moves with the story. The light changes with the time of day the text gives: evening for the upper room, night in Gethsemane, darkness from the sixth hour to the ninth, dawn on the first day of the week. Every Scripture reference opens the passage in the Berean Standard Bible.

How sure is each mark?

Some places are certain, some are known only roughly, and some come from later tradition. The map never shows more certainty than the evidence allows.

  • Named place. The Gospel names the town. The dot is an approximate locator.
  • Region. An area is named, but not the exact site, so the map draws a circle rather than a point.
  • Schematic route. The journey is recorded; the road is not. Lines are drawn as arcs and never follow modern roads.
  • Narrative link. The story moves on, but the travel isn’t described.
  • Reconstruction. A simplified model of something historical, such as the temple courts or the walls of Jerusalem.
  • Traditional or proposed site. Identified by later tradition or by modern proposal, not by the text.
  • Territory. Who ruled where. Boundaries are approximate.

The 3D models

Jerusalem is a simplified model over real terrain. The temple platform, its porticoes and courts, the sanctuary, the Antonia fortress, Herod’s palace and the first wall follow published dimensions, chiefly Josephus and the Mishnah. The houses are illustrative. Only the first wall is shown: the course of the second wall is debated, and the third was begun after the Gospel events.

Capernaum is an illustrative village of one-storey basalt rooms around shared courtyards, the house type excavated there. The synagogue site and the traditional house of Peter are marked. Individual houses and the village’s exact extent are not known.

Scripture and dates

Quotations are from the Berean Standard Bible, which is in the public domain. Narrative text paraphrases the passage cited on each scene. Where the Gospels tell an event differently, the scene says so.

The scenes follow a common harmonized order, and the site notes where that order is an interpretation. Calendar years are given only where there is good evidence, such as Luke’s fifteenth year of Tiberius. Elsewhere the atlas uses the Gospels’ own time markers: “six days before the Passover”, “the third hour”.

Background and evidence

Many scenes have two optional notes. Life in Jesus’ day explains customs the text assumes. Found by archaeologists describes excavations, inscriptions and writers outside the Bible. Each note cites its source; the full list is on the Sources page.

Your privacy

The atlas collects nothing about you: no accounts, no ads, no cookies and no tracking. Your reading progress, saved scenes and settings stay on your own device. To show a passage, play audio or draw the terrain, your browser asks the services named on the Sources page for it, as any web page would.

How it was checked

In October 2026 two reviewers independently checked every scene, and before release a fresh pair read all 90 scenes again, start to finish. One compared every quotation with the Berean Standard Bible and every summary with its passage. The other checked the historical, archaeological and geographical claims against published sources. Their corrections are included. If you find something that should be corrected, please open an issue on GitHub.
